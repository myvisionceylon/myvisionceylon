## the vision

Imagine a day when Sri Lankan engineers can launch satellites into orbit from their own country. A purpose-built spaceport on the eastern coast hosts small-satellite launch vehicles, spacecraft integration facilities, mission control and aerospace research laboratories.

The project begins through a partnership between the government, universities and an established international launch operator. Rather than developing every technology from scratch, Sri Lanka provides the infrastructure, workforce and strategic location while partners bring launch vehicles and operational expertise.

Over time, the facility could grow into a regional aerospace hub supporting satellite manufacturing, testing, communications and commercial launch services. Sri Lanka moves from being solely a customer of space technology to participating in the industry that puts satellites into orbit.

## why here

- **Near the equator:** At approximately 6–10° north, Sri Lanka benefits from Earth's eastward rotation. This provides a modest performance advantage for suitable eastward launches, particularly to low-inclination orbits.
- **Eastern coastline:** An eastward launch corridor over the Indian Ocean could help reduce risks to populated areas, subject to trajectory and debris-risk modelling.
- **Trincomalee harbour:** Existing maritime infrastructure could support the import of oversized rocket components, launch equipment and satellite hardware.
- **Potential for dedicated land:** Locations outside the main harbour and population centres could be investigated for a purpose-built launch complex.
- **Regional opportunity:** Sri Lanka could seek partnerships with international launch companies and satellite operators looking for additional launch capacity.
- **Climate considerations:** Eastern Sri Lanka has seasonal weather patterns that must be assessed using rainfall, cloud, lightning, wind and monsoon data before selecting a site.

Trincomalee is a candidate region, not a confirmed launch site. Other eastern and south-eastern coastal locations, including areas near Panama–Pottuvil and Hambantota, should also be compared.

## pros

- Creates high-skilled employment in aerospace engineering, electronics, software and satellite systems.
- Potential foreign income from commercial launch services and related aerospace activities.
- Encourages investment in science, engineering education and university research.
- Uses Sri Lanka's geographical position to gain a modest advantage for selected orbital missions.
- Could support satellite assembly, spacecraft testing, ground stations and communications infrastructure.
- Builds expertise in technologies useful for weather forecasting, disaster monitoring, agriculture and maritime observation.
- Could attract international technology partners and establish a new high-tech industrial sector.
- Gives Sri Lanka an opportunity to participate in the growing global space economy.

## cons

- **Very high upfront investment:** A commercial orbital launch facility needs specialised launch pads, integration buildings, tracking systems, safety infrastructure and utilities. Total costs require a feasibility study.
- **Limited initial demand:** Sri Lanka would need to attract customers in an industry with established competitors and existing launch infrastructure.
- **Environmental risks:** Construction and launches could affect coastal habitats, fisheries, marine ecosystems and wildlife. Protected areas and sensitive habitats must be mapped and avoided.
- **Public safety:** Launch failures, explosions, hazardous propellants and falling debris require extensive safety zones and emergency-response arrangements.
- **Booster recovery:** Reusable rockets require suitable landing zones or specialised recovery vessels, along with tracking, transport, inspection and refurbishment facilities.
- **Weather disruptions:** Monsoons, thunderstorms, lightning, strong winds and tropical cyclones could restrict launch windows.
- **Logistics costs:** Existing port access is useful, but specialised road transport, secure storage, utilities and launch infrastructure would still be needed.
- **International regulation:** The project would require an appropriate licensing framework, launch liability arrangements, insurance and compliance with international obligations.
- **Regional coordination:** Airspace, shipping routes and launch trajectories would need coordination with relevant authorities and neighbouring countries.
- **Opportunity cost:** Investment in satellite services, research and aerospace education may provide benefits with lower financial and operational risks.

## how it could happen

1. **Phase 1 — Feasibility and site selection (1–2 years)**

   Compare Trincomalee, other eastern coastal locations and Hambantota. Study orbital requirements, launch corridors, land availability, transport links, weather, protected areas, public safety and potential demand. Estimate construction and operating costs.

2. **Phase 2 — Build local space capabilities (2–5 years)**

   Support university satellite projects, ground stations, aerospace research and spacecraft testing. Develop engineering skills and establish relationships with international space organisations before committing to a full launch complex.

3. **Phase 3 — International partnership and infrastructure (5–10 years)**

   If the feasibility study supports the project, secure an experienced launch operator and suitable financing. Develop a dedicated launch site with integration facilities, mission control, safety systems and a recovery strategy appropriate to the selected rocket.

4. **Phase 4 — Commercial launches and industry growth (10+ years)**

   Begin with a limited programme for suitable small-satellite missions. Expand launch frequency and supporting industries only when safety, reliability and commercial demand justify it.

## open questions

- Is Trincomalee the best region, or would another eastern or south-eastern coastal site offer a safer and more economical launch corridor?
- How much payload performance would Sri Lanka's latitude advantage actually provide for the target orbits?
- Is there sufficient international demand to support another small-satellite launch facility?
- Should the project focus on expendable rockets initially, or invest in reusable boosters and sea recovery?
- How much would construction, operations, insurance and maintenance cost?
- Could an international launch company finance and operate the facility through a public-private partnership?
- What would be the impact on coastal communities, fisheries, marine wildlife and protected areas?
- What legal framework and international agreements would Sri Lanka need before allowing orbital launches?
- Would a satellite manufacturing and research hub be a better first investment than a full-scale launch site?
- What measurable economic and scientific benefits would justify the investment?
