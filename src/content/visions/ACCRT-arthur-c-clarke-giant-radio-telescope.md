## the vision

Imagine a giant radio telescope in Sri Lanka, listening to the universe through signals invisible to the human eye.

The Arthur C. Clarke Giant Radio Telescope would be a flagship scientific facility dedicated to radio astronomy. Named in honour of Sir Arthur C. Clarke, who lived in Sri Lanka and helped popularise satellite communications and space science, it would represent a long-term commitment to scientific discovery.

The telescope could investigate pulsars, galaxies, interstellar gas, fast radio bursts and other astronomical phenomena. Depending on the research objectives, it could be a large steerable dish or the first component of a larger antenna array.

The ambition is to establish an internationally collaborative observatory where Sri Lankan scientists and engineers can develop expertise, participate in research programmes and contribute observations to global astronomy.

## why here

- A remote inland site could reduce interference from dense urban and industrial activity.
- The dry-zone interior offers locations worth surveying for terrain, accessibility and environmental suitability.
- A sufficiently large site could accommodate the telescope, control facilities and future expansion.
- Fibre connectivity and reliable power could support the large volumes of scientific data.
- The facility could collaborate with international observatories and universities.

Maduru Oya is a candidate area only. Radio-frequency interference surveys, environmental assessments, land-use studies and engineering investigations must determine whether it is suitable.

## pros

- Establishes a major scientific research facility in Sri Lanka.
- Supports research into galaxies, pulsars and other cosmic phenomena.
- Develops expertise in antenna engineering, low-noise electronics and scientific computing.
- Creates opportunities for students and researchers.
- Attracts international scientific collaborations.
- Could become a flagship project under SERENDIB.

## cons

- High construction and maintenance costs.
- Radio interference from terrestrial transmitters, satellites and electrical equipment.
- Environmental impacts from construction, roads and power infrastructure.
- Limited local expertise in specialised radio astronomy systems.
- Significant ongoing costs for receivers, computing, calibration and operations.
- Competition with established international observatories for funding and research partnerships.

## how it could happen

1. Survey candidate locations for radio interference, weather, terrain and environmental constraints.
2. Establish a smaller research antenna and develop partnerships with universities and international radio astronomy organisations.
3. Determine the required dish size, observing frequencies and scientific programme.
4. Secure funding and build the main telescope after successful site testing.
5. Expand research capabilities and, if justified, develop an interconnected antenna array.

## open questions

- Is Maduru Oya the best location?
- Should ACCRT be a giant single dish or an array?
- Which scientific questions should it prioritise?
- How much would construction and annual operations cost?
- Could Sri Lanka establish effective radio-interference protections around the site?
- What international partners could help design and operate the facility?
